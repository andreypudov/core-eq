#+TITLE: CoreEQ Roadmap
#+SUBTITLE: Fix what is broken, then open the format, then open the scope
#+AUTHOR: Andrey Pudov
#+DATE: <2026-10-18 Sun>
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:nil

Where CoreEQ goes after 1.10, and why in this order. Everything here comes out
of what people asked for and reported after the app was posted publicly; the
shape of the filter chain the later releases build on is in [[file:ARCHITECTURE.org][ARCHITECTURE.org]].

The ordering rule is simple. A defect that makes the app produce no sound
outranks the most-asked-for feature, and both outrank a feature that
widens what the app is. So: 1.8 fixed, 1.9 opened the preset format, and 1.10
opened the scope only as far as leaving apps out, with the AutoEq catalog
alongside. All three plans are done. What is left is the two defects that have
not been reproduced, below, and then *Later*. One chain per application is
there, until it is asked for on its own.

Dates are deliberately absent. This is a project I wrote for myself and put on
GitHub because it turned out to be useful to other people; the order is a
commitment, the schedule is not.

What is already fixed moves to [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]] rather than staying here marked
done. This document is what is still owed, so that its length means something.

* Still open — Make it work everywhere it claims to

Public feedback produced three defects that are not edge cases, and chasing the
first turned up two more that nobody had reported. Three of the five are fixed
and have moved to [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]]: audio broken on multichannel outputs, an
Aggregate or Multi-Output Device silencing the Mac outright, and a refused
permission doing the same while every layer of the app reported success. A
fourth suspicion — that a Bluetooth route change left a window of audio filtered
at the wrong sample rate — was measured and turned out not to happen; it is in
the release notes with the numbers.

1.8 shipped on <2026-09-20 Sun> with the five above settled and the rest of this
section still open. 1.9 shipped on <2026-10-01 Thu> and settled two more:
screen recordings that captured the sound twice, and the documentation that
answered the same questions one comment at a time. Both have moved to
[[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]], with the preset format 1.9 was for.

1.10 settled none of what is left, and was not meant to: it shipped the scope
1.10 was planned for — leaving apps out — and the AutoEq catalog, both in
[[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]].

What is left here: an echo after waking that has not been reproduced, and a
report of noise over Bluetooth. They affect people doing nothing unusual, so by
the ordering rule they still head the next release.

Also for the next release, and not visible to anyone using the app: the review
before 1.10 left the largest files alone rather than split them days before a
tag — =AudioEngine= at about 1,600 lines, =ProfileManager= at 1,000, and the
AutoEq browser, the main window's detail and sidebar views, and the menu bar
controller at 750–870 each. Splitting them along the seams they already have
is the first piece of work after 1.10, before anything new is added to them.
The first new thing after that is Shortcuts, under *Later*: small, and
touching none of the audio path.

** Bluetooth noise at higher volume

One report, unresolved: a Soundcore Q20i picking up noise around 40% volume.

Clipping is the first hypothesis and the cheapest to rule out, since =AutoGain=
corrects the *average* of the chain response and never boosts — by design, and
documented as such — so it removes the systematic part of the headroom problem
without guaranteeing clipping away. The mix level arriving at a tap is
unknowable from inside it. If the noise is also present at Flat, it is not
clipping and needs a real investigation.

*Measured, and attributed — but not shown on screen.* An on-screen warning was
built and then taken out again. Two things decided it.

The first was accuracy. It counted any output sample above full scale, and so it
fired at Flat with Auto, which is the default state. At Flat every band is 0 dB,
which =Biquad.isActive= maps to identity, so the chain is skipped and CoreEQ
passes audio through bit for bit; the system mix is Float32 and is not clamped
before a tap sees it, so material arrives over full scale on its own — lossy
codecs overshoot, resampling reconstructs peaks above the samples that made
them, and modern masters sit at the ceiling to begin with. The warning was
blaming the app for the recording, and pointing at a preamp that Auto had
disabled.

That part is fixed and kept: a sample only counts when the chain lifted it past
both full scale *and* the level it arrived at, measured where =BufferRouter=
copies the source, so it costs nothing and reads exactly the samples that become
the output. The report carries both peaks, and says so when the material itself
arrived hot.

The second was whether to show it at all. Preventing this is the convention for
consumer equalizers — AutoEq ships a preamp with every profile for exactly this
reason — and CoreEQ already prevents it with =AutoGain=, on by default. A clip
light is the *professional* convention, and it belongs on an output meter, which
this app does not have. It would be seen rarely, by users who had never seen it
before, in an app whose case for existing is that it is simple. So the
measurement stays and the badge does not.

*The report also answers this without a round trip.* The render path keeps the
largest magnitude it has produced since the engine started and counts the
samples that did not fit, and the diagnostics report carries both — =peak
output: 0.71, no clipping=, or the peak with a count and what to do about it.
Until now the only way to tell was to ask the reporter to switch to Flat and
come back, which is two exchanges to answer a question their own machine
already knew. Next time this is reported, ask for the report first.

** Echo and doubled audio

Two symptoms that sound like one another are left of the three this began with:

- Running alongside SoundSource produces a flangey delay.
- After waking from sleep, audio sometimes plays with an echo.

The third — screen recordings with “a Doppler echo delay” — was measured and
answered in 1.9, and is in [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]]. The recorder hears each app's
sound and CoreEQ's equalized copy of it; nothing loops, and nothing leaked.

A fourth symptom was listed here — the Audio4C reports where sound starts and
then vanishes — and has been removed. It was the channel layout bug in
*Multichannel outputs* above, not doubling, and is fixed. It is named here so
nobody reads it back as evidence for this item.

The SoundSource case has an easy story: two processors in one path, so the
signal arrives twice slightly offset. The wake-from-sleep case does not — no
second tool is involved, so something on CoreEQ's side is producing two copies
of the audio by itself. The likely shape is an IO proc or an aggregate device
that survives a state change it should not have survived, leaving two of them
running. That would explain the echo after wake.

The feedback shape is not hypothetical, and it is worth writing down because it
was observed accidentally. While verifying the multichannel work, a test harness
tapped system audio and wrote its own output back to the same device without
excluding itself from the tap. It captured what it had just written, and the
level compounded across the run — a 0.4 peak measured 1.56, then 26, then 71.
=AudioEngine= excludes its own process for exactly this reason, and since 1.9
refuses to build a tap at all when it cannot identify that process — the lookup
used to fail silently, which the release notes record.

What is left is the wake case:

- Audit the teardown and rebuild path around sleep, wake, and device changes,
  and confirm exactly one IO proc is ever running. The report names how many
  restarts a session has had, why the last one happened, and how long ago, and
  since 1.9 how many CoreEQ aggregates are alive — more than one is
  two paths rendering the same audio. On the development machine it reads one,
  including across the rebuild that follows proving the tap.

  *Not reproduced.* One sleep and wake on a MacBook Pro's built-in speakers,
  macOS 27.0.1, at Flat — the preset where two copies would be loudest —
  played clicks and music clean afterwards. The report agreed: one restart,
  =system woke from sleep=, one aggregate alive, capturing and muting, peak in
  equal to peak out. So neither suspect showed: no path survived the rebuild,
  and the tap did not hear CoreEQ.

  One clean cycle does not settle a report that says “sometimes”. Still worth
  running: audio playing into the sleep, so playback resumes while the path is
  rebuilt; a sleep of ten minutes or more, long enough for the hardware to be
  powered down; and Bluetooth headphones, whose route is renegotiated on wake.
  Until then, the next echo report settles it, as long as the diagnostics
  report comes with it: =audio paths= above one is a leaked path, the
  self-exclusion error is a tap that could not leave CoreEQ out, and an echo
  with neither points somewhere not yet looked at.

* Later — worth doing, not yet scheduled

** Shortcuts

Actions for the Shortcuts app, through App Intents, which macOS also offers in
Spotlight and as Focus filters at no extra cost. Next after the file split, and
deliberately small.

Where it earns its place:

- *Turning the EQ off and on from the keyboard.* Shortcuts can bind any
  shortcut to a global key, so a toggle gives an instant A/B against the
  untouched sound without opening the menu. The strongest case of the lot.
- *Focus filters.* A Work focus picks a flat preset, a Gaming one a V shape: a
  rule CoreEQ does not have, which the system then owns, with no settings pane
  to build.
- *Automations.* On macOS 26 Shortcuts runs them on the Mac — at a time, when an
  app opens, when a display connects — so whatever the actions expose can be
  scheduled without CoreEQ growing rules of its own.
- *Scripting.* =shortcuts run= from a shell, Raycast, or Alfred.

Where it adds little, and why the set stays small: a preset per output device
already covers the commonest automation anyone would build (“when my headphones
connect, use this”), and leaving an app out is exclusion, which works per
process and is better than switching the whole EQ when the app opens.

The first set, three actions:

1. *Turn EQ on, off, or toggle.*
2. *Set preset*, chosen from the user's presets, optionally for a given output
   device.
3. *Get the current preset and whether the EQ is on*, so a shortcut can branch.

The A/B slot is a fourth if it is asked for.

What it costs and what it risks:

- App Intents needs macOS 13, inside the 14.2 floor. Actions run inside the
  running app, which suits a menu bar app, and can launch it in the background
  when it is not running.
- The work is the preset list for the picker, and routing each action through
  the same calls the menu bar makes, so the menu, the window, and Shortcuts
  cannot disagree about what happened. The actions stay thin; the tests cover
  the managers they call. Their names go in the String Catalog.
- None of it touches the audio path. The real risk is that it is public
  surface: once people have built automations on an action, renaming or
  removing it breaks them. Another reason to start with three.

** Per-application EQ and volume

Asked for repeatedly, on Reddit and as GitHub issues; one request puts the
appeal well: per-app volume “would really compliment the eq feature”. Most of
what was asked for under that heading was really exclusion — leave this app
alone — which shipped in 1.10 (see [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]]) without any of what follows.

A curve per app is a bigger change than this document once said. It was
described here as a state-model and UI question, not an audio-path one, because
=CATapDescription= takes a process list. But one tap delivers its processes
already mixed, so a chain per app means a tap per app, each filtered on its own
and summed in the render callback: more Core Audio objects to keep consistent
through device changes, sleep and launches, which is where 1.8's defects lived.

What would have to be decided:

- How a per-app chain relates to the global one. Replace, or stack on top.
- How profiles bind to an application rather than a process. On macOS 26 the
  tap's bundle identifiers and process restore answer this, as they do for
  exclusion.
- Whether per-app implies a mixer view, and how that stays an app someone opens
  to make their music sound better rather than becoming an audio workstation.

Per-app *volume* rides along: a gain stage on a chain that would then exist.

** Rendering through Aggregate and Multi-Output Devices

Refusing them with a clear message is honest, but it is still a no, and the 1.8
fix above changes only what the app does about not being able to.

Supporting them, without giving up the driver-free design, means not wrapping
the user's aggregate at all: read its sub-device list and build an equivalent
*flat* aggregate out of those leaf devices directly, carrying over the main
sub-device, the drift compensation, and whether it is stacked. Nesting is the
only thing Core Audio prohibits; the leaves are ordinary devices. Measured:
rebuilding the same aggregate from its own leaves gives two active sub-devices
and a real output layout, where nesting gave zero of both.

The two kinds then split, and unevenly:

- *Multi-Output Devices* need nothing further. Rebuilt as a stacked aggregate,
  Core Audio does the duplication internally and presents =1 buffer(s) [2ch]= —
  plain stereo, which the render path already handles. This is both the larger
  population and the cheaper half.
- *Aggregate Devices* concatenate their sub-devices' channels, so a flat rebuild
  of two stereo devices presents four. That needed the multichannel layout work
  above, which is now done, so this is unblocked as well.

The open risk is whether two aggregates can run against the same leaf devices at
once. Creation succeeds while the user's own aggregate exists and holds them;
whether both can be *started* has not been tested, and that is the question that
decides the design.

** A/B comparison export

Both slots already hold complete states, so writing out both curves and their
settings is mostly plumbing. Suggested by someone who has built the feature
elsewhere and offered to help.

** Live waveform view

There is already an FFT feeding the spectrum analyser; a time-domain view is a
second read of the same buffer, tied to the A/B toggle.

** Preset recommendation

“Please recommend a configuration or built-in option” read two ways: tell me
which to pick, or pick for me. The 1.9 preset descriptions answer the first
cheaply. The second needs the app to know the device and the material, which is
worth thinking about rather than guessing at.

* Not planned

Stated here so the answer is written down once.

- *Bit-perfect passthrough.* A process tap sits after the system mixer by
  definition, so the honest maximum CoreEQ can offer is getting out of the path
  entirely, which bypass already does — and, since 1.10, per app, by leaving it
  out of the tap. Real bit-perfect means hog mode and exclusive device access,
  which is a different application. Better to not claim it than to half-claim
  it.
- *Global volume control.* macOS already has one. CoreEQ adjusts tone.
- *HRTF.* Raised once with no detail. Large, and a different product from an
  equalizer.
- *A standalone audio-tap-to-file tool.* Suggested as an unmet need, and the tap
  plumbing here is most of the work, but it is not this app.
- *iOS.* The process-tap API is macOS-only, so this is not a port.

* How this was chosen

Three sources, each read through in full: the r/MacApps launch thread, from
people actually running the app; GitHub issues; and the earlier Threads post,
from people reacting to a screenshot, whose top request — parametric bands —
shipped in 1.6.

Reddit and GitHub reports are not deduplicated, and probably should not be
taken as separate voices: someone who hits a bug in the thread is exactly the
person who then files the issue. Where this document counts anything, it counts
distinct symptoms or broken configurations rather than people.

The pattern across the feedback is worth keeping in view: the requests cluster around
the preset *format* and the *scope* of the chain, not around the DSP. The
filtering is not what people want changed.
