#+TITLE: CoreEQ Roadmap
#+SUBTITLE: Fix what is broken, then open the format, then open the scope
#+AUTHOR: Andrey Pudov
#+DATE: <2026-08-30 Sun>
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:nil

Where CoreEQ goes after 1.7, and why in this order. Everything here comes out
of what people asked for and reported after the app was posted publicly; the
shape of the filter chain the later releases build on is in [[file:ARCHITECTURE.org][ARCHITECTURE.org]].

The ordering rule is simple. A defect that makes the app produce no sound
outranks the most-asked-for feature, and both outrank a feature that
widens what the app is. So: 1.8 fixes, 1.9 opens the preset format, 1.10 opens
the scope from one chain per device to one chain per application.

Dates are deliberately absent. This is a project I wrote for myself and put on
GitHub because it turned out to be useful to other people; the order is a
commitment, the schedule is not.

What is already fixed moves to [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]] rather than staying here marked
done. This document is what is still owed, so that its length means something.

* 1.8 — Make it work everywhere it claims to

Public feedback produced three defects that are not edge cases, and chasing the
first turned up two more that nobody had reported. Three of the five are fixed
and have moved to [[file:RELEASE_NOTES.org][RELEASE_NOTES.org]]: audio broken on multichannel outputs, an
Aggregate or Multi-Output Device silencing the Mac outright, and a refused
permission doing the same while every layer of the app reported success. A
fourth suspicion — that a Bluetooth route change left a window of audio filtered
at the wrong sample rate — was measured and turned out not to happen; it is in
the release notes with the numbers.

What is left here: audio arriving twice and every screen capture tool failing, a
Mac kept awake indefinitely, and two smaller reports. They affect people doing
nothing unusual, none can be worked around from inside the app, and nothing else
ships until they are fixed.

** Bluetooth noise at higher volume

One report, unresolved: a Soundcore Q20i picking up noise around 40% volume.

Clipping is the first hypothesis and the cheapest to rule out, since =AutoGain=
corrects the *average* of the chain response and never boosts — by design, and
documented as such — so it removes the systematic part of the headroom problem
without guaranteeing clipping away. The mix level arriving at a tap is
unknowable from inside it. If the noise is also present at Flat, it is not
clipping and needs a real investigation.

** Echo and doubled audio

Three symptoms that sound like one another, and the second is the one that makes
this a defect rather than an explanation:

- Running alongside SoundSource produces a flangey delay.
- With CoreEQ active, screen recording fails in *every* capture tool tested, and
  the captured audio has “a Doppler echo delay”. Reported twice.
- After waking from sleep, audio sometimes plays with an echo.

A fourth symptom was listed here — the Audio4C reports where sound starts and
then vanishes — and has been removed. It was the channel layout bug in
*Multichannel outputs* above, not doubling, and is fixed. It is named here so
nobody reads it back as evidence for this item.

The SoundSource case has an easy story: two processors in one path, so the
signal arrives twice slightly offset. The wake-from-sleep case does not — no
second tool is involved, so something on CoreEQ's side is producing two copies
of the audio by itself. The likely shape is an IO proc or an aggregate device
that survives a state change it should not have survived, leaving two of them
running. That would explain the echo after wake, and it is worth testing
whether it also explains the capture-tool case.

The feedback shape is not hypothetical, and it is worth writing down because it
was observed accidentally. While verifying the multichannel work, a test harness
tapped system audio and wrote its own output back to the same device without
excluding itself from the tap. It captured what it had just written, and the
level compounded across the run — a 0.4 peak measured 1.56, then 26, then 71.
=AudioEngine= excludes its own process for exactly this reason. Two taps in one
path, one of them playing back what the other captures, is the same shape, and
that is what a capture tool and CoreEQ are doing to each other.

Three things to do:

- Audit the teardown and rebuild path around sleep, wake, and device changes,
  and confirm exactly one IO proc is ever running.
- Reproduce against a screen capture tool. Capture tools take their own tap of
  the system audio, so CoreEQ's global tap and theirs are interacting.
  “Every capture tool fails” makes this a real conflict, not a quirk of one app.
- When a second system-audio tool genuinely is present, detect it and say so
  plainly in the UI rather than leaving the user to work out why their audio
  sounds doubled.

** Preventing the Mac from sleeping

Activity Monitor shows CoreEQ preventing sleep, and it does so with no audio
playing at all.

The cause is not an assertion in CoreEQ's own code — there isn't one. It is the
IO proc: =AudioDeviceStart= runs the aggregate continuously from launch, by
design, since global bypass “keeps the engine running but passes audio through”
(=AudioEngine.swift:49=). A continuously running IOProc is active audio IO as
far as the system is concerned, so the assertion follows automatically.

*That explanation is wrong, and the real one is narrower.* Measured while
investigating the route-change window: with the engine running and nothing
playing, =RateTrace= recorded *no IO cycles at all* over twenty-two seconds, on
the Jabra and again on the built-in speakers. The render callback is not being
called. So it is not a continuously running IOProc.

=pmset -g assertions=, taken at the same moment, names what it actually is:

#+begin_example
pid 401(coreaudiod): PreventUserIdleSystemSleep named:
  "com.apple.audio.com.andreypudov.coreeq.aggregate.<uid>.context.preventuseridlesleep"
  Created for PID: 36385.
  Resources: audio-in audio-out BuiltInSpeakerDevice
#+end_example

The assertion belongs to *coreaudiod*, raised for CoreEQ's aggregate device and
attributed back to CoreEQ's pid — which is why Activity Monitor blames an app
whose own source contains no assertion, and why searching that source for one
found nothing. It is held because the aggregate has been *started*, not because
audio is flowing: it was there with zero callbacks in twenty-two seconds.

That changes the fix. Making the IO path idle-aware achieves nothing, because it
is already idle. The assertion goes away only by stopping the aggregate
(=AudioDeviceStop=) when nothing is playing and starting it again on demand,
which is a real design change: something has to notice playback beginning, the
restart has to be fast enough not to clip the first moment of it, and the tap's
mute behaviour has to survive the cycle. Worth doing, and worth its own design
rather than an afternoon.

The fix is to stop running when there is nothing to do: tear the IO proc down
when the app is bypassed, and when the mix has been silent for long enough that
restarting on the next sample is cheaper than holding the machine awake. The
constraint is that starting back up has to be fast and seamless enough that the
first second of playback is not lost.

** Documentation that answers the repeated questions

Cheap, and it closes three of the recurring threads:

- A one-line description per preset, so the list does not assume the reader
  already knows what Acoustic or Spoken Word is for.
- A =/Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/HAL= listing as the way to verify the no-driver
  claim independently. A commenter proposed this unprompted, and it is better
  proof than any wording I could write.
- A stated-limits section in the README covering global volume, multichannel,
  and bit-perfect playback, so the deliberate non-goals stop being answered one
  comment at a time.

* 1.9 — Open the preset format

The most requested feature by a clear margin, asked for from four directions:
AutoEQ, EqualizerAPO, Wavelet, and “EQ txt files”. All four describe the same
file.

** Import

AutoEQ's parametric export gives a preamp value and a frequency/gain/Q triple
per band. EqualizerAPO consumes that format and Wavelet ships the same
database, so one parser covers every request in the thread.

The chain already expresses what the format says: free filters take any centre
in =filterFrequencyRange= (20 Hz – 20 kHz) with Q from 0.1 to 10, and the
profile's preamp maps onto the existing output trim. So the work is a parser, a
file picker, and three decisions the format forces:

- A profile with more than =maxFreeFilters= (16) bands. Truncating by
  significance beats refusing the file.
- A band past the ±12 dB =gainRange=. Clamp and say so, rather than silently.
- Whether an imported profile replaces the ladder or sits on top of it. Replace
  is almost certainly right: a correction curve is a complete statement about
  the headphone, not an adjustment to an existing one.

** Export

Asked for once, alongside import, and nearly free once the parser exists.
Writing the same format back makes a CoreEQ profile something you can hand to
someone on another platform.

** Done when

An AutoEQ profile downloaded for a real pair of headphones loads and measures
correct against its published response, and what CoreEQ writes out reloads
identically.

* 1.10 — Open the scope: per-application EQ and volume

Asked for repeatedly, on Reddit and as GitHub issues, and someone also asked
whether the process-tap API supports per-app taps at all. One request puts the
appeal well: per-app volume “would really compliment the eq feature”.

It does. =CATapDescription= takes a process list; CoreEQ only uses the
global-minus-itself variant because I wanted one curve over everything. So this
is not an audio-path question. It is a state-model question — one chain per
process rather than one chain per device — and a UI question, which is the
larger half.

What has to be decided:

- How a per-app chain relates to the global one. Replace, or stack on top.
- What happens to a chain when its application quits and comes back with a new
  pid, and how profiles bind to an application rather than a process.
- Whether per-app implies a mixer view, and how that stays an app someone opens
  to make their music sound better rather than becoming an audio workstation.

Per-app *volume* is the easier half and rides along: it is a gain stage on a
chain that already exists.

* Later — worth doing, not yet scheduled

** Rendering through Aggregate and Multi-Output Devices

Refusing them with a clear message is honest, but it is still a no, and the 1.8
fix above changes only what the app does about not being able to.

Supporting them, without giving up the driver-free design, means not wrapping
the user's aggregate at all: read its sub-device list and build an equivalent
*flat* aggregate out of those leaf devices directly, carrying over the main
sub-device, the drift compensation, and whether it is stacked. Nesting is the
only thing Core Audio prohibits; the leaves are ordinary devices. Measured:
rebuilding the same aggregate from its own leaves gives two active sub-devices
and a real output layout, where nesting gave zero of both.

The two kinds then split, and unevenly:

- *Multi-Output Devices* need nothing further. Rebuilt as a stacked aggregate,
  Core Audio does the duplication internally and presents =1 buffer(s) [2ch]= —
  plain stereo, which the render path already handles. This is both the larger
  population and the cheaper half.
- *Aggregate Devices* concatenate their sub-devices' channels, so a flat rebuild
  of two stereo devices presents four. That needed the multichannel layout work
  above, which is now done, so this is unblocked as well.

The open risk is whether two aggregates can run against the same leaf devices at
once. Creation succeeds while the user's own aggregate exists and holds them;
whether both can be *started* has not been tested, and that is the question that
decides the design.

** A/B comparison export

Both slots already hold complete states, so writing out both curves and their
settings is mostly plumbing. Suggested by someone who has built the feature
elsewhere and offered to help.

** Live waveform view

There is already an FFT feeding the spectrum analyser; a time-domain view is a
second read of the same buffer, tied to the A/B toggle.

** Loading presets from the AutoEQ database

Requested, naming [[https://autoeq.app][autoeq.app]] as the source.

The step past file import: search the community database by headphone model and
apply it, the way Poweramp does. Open data, so the work is a network fetch plus
the parser 1.9 already has to write.

*Automatic* is worth pinning down before any of this is built, because it reads
two ways. Fetching a profile without the user downloading a file by hand is this
item, and is well defined. Choosing a profile for whatever headphones happen to
be connected is *Preset recommendation* below, and needs the app to identify the
device rather than the user to name it — a different and much larger problem.

The two readings also differ on privacy, which is the part that decides the
shape. This would be CoreEQ's first outbound network connection, and "nothing is
recorded, stored, or transmitted" is a claim worth keeping. A fetch the user
asks for by searching a model name is compatible with that and can be stated
plainly. A fetch that happens on its own — on launch, or whenever a device
appears — is not, whatever it is fetching. So if this ships, it ships on explicit
user action, and the wording in the app says so.

** Preset recommendation

“Please recommend a configuration or built-in option” read two ways: tell me
which to pick, or pick for me. The 1.8 preset descriptions answer the first
cheaply. The second needs the app to know the device and the material, which is
worth thinking about rather than guessing at.

* Not planned

Stated here so the answer is written down once.

- *Bit-perfect passthrough.* A process tap sits after the system mixer by
  definition, so the honest maximum CoreEQ can offer is getting out of the path
  entirely, which bypass already does. Real bit-perfect means hog mode and
  exclusive device access, which is a different application. Better to not
  claim it than to half-claim it.
- *Global volume control.* macOS already has one. CoreEQ adjusts tone.
- *HRTF.* Raised once with no detail. Large, and a different product from an
  equalizer.
- *A standalone audio-tap-to-file tool.* Suggested as an unmet need, and the tap
  plumbing here is most of the work, but it is not this app.
- *iOS.* The process-tap API is macOS-only, so this is not a port.

* How this was chosen

Three sources, each read through in full: the r/MacApps launch thread, from
people actually running the app; GitHub issues; and the earlier Threads post,
from people reacting to a screenshot, whose top request — parametric bands —
shipped in 1.6.

Reddit and GitHub reports are not deduplicated, and probably should not be
taken as separate voices: someone who hits a bug in the thread is exactly the
person who then files the issue. Where this document counts anything, it counts
distinct symptoms or broken configurations rather than people.

The pattern across the feedback is worth keeping in view: the requests cluster around
the preset *format* and the *scope* of the chain, not around the DSP. The
filtering is not what people want changed.
