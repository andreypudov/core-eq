#+TITLE: CoreEQ Architecture Proposal
#+SUBTITLE: One filter chain, three depths of access
#+DATE: <2026-08-06 Thu>
#+OPTIONS: toc:3 num:nil

* Goal

CoreEQ needs parametric editing — arbitrary centre frequency and bandwidth is
the single most requested change in [[file:COMMENTS_ANALYSIS.org][COMMENTS_ANALYSIS.org]], and headphone
correction and AutoEQ import are unimplementable without it. It also needs to
stay an application a person opens to make their music sound better, not an
audio workstation.

Those two goals only conflict if parametric editing is treated as a second
equalizer that has to coexist with the first. This proposal removes the
conflict by not having a second equalizer.

* The model

There is one equalizer: a chain of biquad filters.

Eleven of those filters have their frequency and Q locked to the ISO octave
ladder. Those are the sliders. Any other filter in the chain is free — the user
chooses its type, frequency, gain, and Q.

Both kinds are the same object, processed by the same engine, drawn on the same
graph. "Graphic" and "parametric" are not two equalizers, two modes, or two
layers. They are two ways of reaching filters in one chain.

** Filter

#+begin_src text
Filter
{
    Type          bell | low shelf | high shelf | high pass | low pass
    Frequency
    Gain
    Q
    Enabled
    Band          ladder slot index, or none
}
#+end_src

=Band= is what makes a filter a slider. A filter with a slot index is drawn in
the slider strip; its frequency comes from =BuiltInProfiles.frequencies= at
that index and its Q from =BuiltInProfiles.defaultQ=, and neither is editable.
A filter without one is drawn in the filter list and on the graph as a free
node.

The slot index — not the frequency — is the identity of a band, so the slider
strip has a stable mapping into a variable-length array.

** Preset

#+begin_src text
Preset
{
    Name
    Filters
}
#+end_src

That is the whole preset. A built-in preset is eleven band filters carrying
that preset's gains. =Flat= is eleven band filters at 0 dB, which is to say
nothing at all — a 0 dB filter is already identity in =PeakingFilter= and costs
no processing.

Identity stays the name, as today. There is no UUID — nothing in this proposal
needs preset identity to change, and changing it would cost a migration for no
benefit.

* DSP pipeline

#+begin_src text
Preset filters
      │
      ▼
  enabled, non-identity
      │
      ▼
  flat [Filter] array
      │
      ▼
   EQProcessor
      │
      ├──▶ Frequency response
      │
      ▼
  Audio output
#+end_src

One flattening step, in one place, producing one array. =EQProcessor= receives
it exactly the way it receives bands today and has no knowledge of slots,
sliders, or lists. Filters are cascaded, so their magnitudes multiply and their
dB contributions add exactly. The response curve is arithmetic, not an
approximation, and filter order has no effect on either magnitude or phase.

The chain is minimum phase. This is the expected and correct choice for a
system equalizer; linear-phase processing is a separate mode and out of scope
here.

* Three depths of access

The same chain is reachable at three levels. Most users only ever use the
first.

** Menu bar

Master switch, preset list, response preview, bass/mid/treble, output device.
Unchanged. Filters never appear here — this is the surface for someone who
wants their music to sound better and nothing else.

** Window

Preset menu, response graph, eleven sliders, output device. Unchanged from
today. This is what opens, and it is what a user who knows the Apple Music or
Spotify equalizer already knows how to operate.

** Filters

One collapsed row at the bottom of =EqualizerDetailView=, below the sliders:

#+begin_src text
 Band Levels                                    ⏻
 ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐  ▐
 32 64 125 250 500 1k 2k 4k 8k 16k 20k

 ▸ Filters — None
#+end_src

Expanded:

#+begin_src text
 ▾ Filters                                   ⏻  +
   ● Bell         180 Hz    −3.0 dB    Q 1.20
   ● High Shelf     8 kHz   −2.0 dB    Q 0.70
#+end_src

Collapsed by default, state remembered. A user who never opens it sees the
application exactly as it is now, plus one quiet line.

Once someone expands the section they have opted in, so the controls inside use
the standard vocabulary — Bell, Low Shelf, High Shelf, High Pass, Low Pass, and
Q labelled Q. Renaming Q to something friendlier helps nobody: the casual user
still does not want it, and the knowledgeable user then has to guess whether it
means Q, bandwidth, or octaves. The deep end is hidden well rather than made
shallow.

Notch and band-pass are deliberately omitted. Every filter type is a
coefficient formula that has to be mirrored exactly between the audio path and
the plot, and neither type earns that cost in a listening application.

* The graph

The graph always shows the complete response of the chain — the sum of every
enabled filter, band and free alike. That curve is what reaches the speakers.

** Two kinds of point

Band handles sit at the eleven ladder positions and move vertically only. Free
filter nodes move in both axes, with a modifier drag or scroll for Q.

The constraint is the explanation: a band handle physically cannot be dragged
sideways, so the difference between a locked band and a free filter is learned
from the mouse rather than from a label. They should look different as well —
band handles small and on the ladder positions, filter nodes larger and ringed.

** Creating filters from the graph

While the Filters section is collapsed the graph behaves exactly as it does
today, and a filter cannot be created by accident. While it is expanded,
double-clicking empty graph space creates a bell at that frequency and gain and
adds it to the list.

** Double-click

Double-click keeps its existing meaning — reset this point's gain to 0 dB — for
both kinds of point. Most parametric editors delete a node on double-click;
consistency inside CoreEQ is worth more than matching that convention, and an
accidental reset is recoverable in a way an accidental delete is not. Delete
lives in the node's context menu and in the list row.

* Rules that keep it predictable

These are the guarantees the design exists to provide.

1. A slider shows the gain of its own filter, and nothing else. It never moves
   on its own.
2. The graph shows the sum of the whole chain. It is exact.
3. Where those two differ, a faint tick on the slider track marks the total at
   that frequency.
4. A preset sets the entire chain.
5. Nothing is ever fitted, estimated, or redistributed.

** The tick

#+begin_src text
   +12 ┤
       │
    +6 ┤   ▐   ← knob: this band is +6.0 dB
       │   ┄   ← tick: the chain totals +8.0 dB here
     0 ┤   ▐
#+end_src

A slider that reports the total instead of its own value would be a control
that lies. Adding a free filter near 125 Hz would slide the 125 Hz slider by
itself; a high pass below it would make the top of the slider's own scale
unreachable; and dragging the slider would require solving for the gain that
produces a requested total, which is exactly the kind of inverse problem this
design exists to avoid.

The tick gives the same information — what is happening at this frequency —
without any of that. Both values are visible on one control and only one of
them is draggable. An untouched band reads as "I am contributing nothing here,
but something else is."

* Crossing over

A band is a filter with two parameters locked. Unlocking is therefore not a
conversion but a change of one field: clear the slot index, and the filter
leaves the slider strip for the list with its frequency, gain, and Q intact.
The coefficients are unchanged, so the sound is identical across the operation.

This is offered as *Edit as filter* in a slider's context menu, and it is the
direct answer to the most requested feature — "I like what 125 Hz is doing, but
I want it narrower."

The reverse is not offered. Collapsing free filters back onto eleven fixed
slots is a curve fit: an optimizer, a residual, and a result that only
approximates what the user had. Moving one filter between presentations is
exact; fitting many filters into a fixed ladder is not, and only the exact
operation belongs in this application.

* Presets

A preset is the complete sound. Selecting one replaces the whole chain,
including any free filters, and built-in presets carry none. =Flat= is
therefore flat.

The alternative — presets that set only the eleven bands and leave free filters
in place — makes every preset name a lie for as long as any filter exists. That
trades a one-time confirmation prompt for permanent untrustworthiness.

Switching away from an edited chain uses the existing unsaved-changes path:
=ProfileManager.isModified= already covers slider edits and extends to filters
without change. Saving a user preset captures the whole chain.

* Why not layers or modes

An earlier draft of this document proposed a Graphic Layer and a Parametric
Layer edited through separate modes. Both ideas are dropped.

*Modes.* The stated aim is that a user never wonders which equalizer is active.
Two tabs guarantee they will, because a mode is a thing you have to keep track
of. Eleven sliders and a short filter list fit on one screen, so nothing is
gained by hiding either behind the other.

*Layers.* Two arrays summed by the engine is a correct model, but the summing
is the only thing it adds — and a single array already sums. The layer split
also forces a wholesale ban on moving filters between layers, which throws away
the exact, useful case described above along with the lossy one.

*Conversion between two equalizers.* Rejected for the reason the earlier draft
gave, and it remains the right rejection. Fitting a free filter set onto a fixed
ladder is an approximation, and approximations are what this application is
trying not to make.

* Impact on the code

The engine is nearly unaffected, which is the strongest evidence the model is
right.

- =EQBand= becomes =Filter=: adds =type=, =enabled=, and the slot index.
- =PeakingFilter= becomes a typed biquad. Its defining property must survive
  unchanged: one coefficient implementation, used by both =EQProcessor= and
  =FrequencyResponseView=. This is what makes the drawn curve a truthful
  picture of the audio path, and every new filter type is a new opportunity to
  break it.
- =EQProfile= holds =[Filter]= instead of =[EQBand]=. Existing user presets
  decode into eleven band filters; the migration is a decoder default.
- =EQProcessor.maxBands= rises from 16. Eleven slots plus a cap of sixteen free
  filters plus room for future automatic processing suggests 32. Each filter is
  one more pass over the buffer in =processChannel=, so the cap is a real
  budget rather than a formality.
- =EqualizerDetailView= gains one collapsible section between the sliders and
  the output row.
- =FrequencyResponseView= gains free nodes alongside the band handles, and
  two-axis dragging for them.
- =QuickTone= is unaffected: it offsets band gains by slot, and slots still
  exist.

* Deferred

Out of scope here, but the shape above should not have to change to accept
them.

- *Preamp and headroom.* Two filters boosting the same frequency compounds, and
  the first user to stack a band and a free filter will clip. The preset schema
  should reserve the field now even if nothing sets it, so this does not become
  a second migration.
- *Ladder Q.* At Q 1.41 and octave spacing, eleven equal slider settings do not
  produce a flat curve — the response peaks at the centres and sags between
  them. Because CoreEQ draws the true response rather than a decorative spline,
  that ripple is visible on screen. It is the one detail that undercuts the
  claim that nothing here is approximate, and it is the first thing a
  knowledgeable user tests.
- *Automatic processing.* Headphone correction, AutoEQ import, room correction.
  These arrive as filters with a flag marking them as machine-generated, which
  the model above already accommodates. Their value to a casual user is that
  presets become better, not that anyone edits a filter table.
- *Linear phase.* A second processing mode, not a change to this model.

* Summary

One chain of filters. Eleven of them locked to the ISO ladder and drawn as
sliders; the rest free and drawn as a list. One graph showing the exact sum.
Three depths of access, with the shallowest unchanged from today.

A casual user sees the equalizer they already know. A knowledgeable user opens
one section and finds a filter chain with honest numbers and a truthful curve.
Neither is asked to think about layers, modes, or conversion, because there
are none.
