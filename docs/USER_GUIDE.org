#+TITLE: CoreEQ — User Guide
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:nil

How to use CoreEQ, and what each part of it does. Installing it is covered in
the [[file:../README.org][README]].

* Getting started

1. Launch CoreEQ. A sliders icon appears in the menu bar; there is no Dock icon.
2. Allow *System Audio Recording*. On first launch CoreEQ opens its window and
   explains what the permission is for before macOS asks; press *Allow…* and
   macOS shows its prompt. CoreEQ needs the permission to process the sound you
   hear. Nothing is recorded, stored, or transmitted. See [[*The audio permission][The audio permission]].
3. Choose a preset. The sound changes immediately.

* The window

Presets in the sidebar, with a search field and your own presets above the
built-ins, and a bar beneath them: the =+= creates a preset or imports one, and
*AutoEq* opens the catalog or the guide to autoeq.app. Then the response
graph, the *Graphic* / *Parametric* editors, and the preamp on the right. The
output device sits in the centre of the header and names the preset playing on
it underneath, with a dot when that preset has unsaved changes; where the
machine has more than one output, that control is also how you choose between
them. *Revert* sits beside it — next to the preset it would discard — and the
gear and the master switch close the row.

** One filter chain, two ways to edit it

The *Graphic* editor is the professional ISO octave ladder — 32, 64, 125, 250,
500 Hz and 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 20 kHz — at fixed centre frequencies, each adjustable
±12 dB. The *Parametric* editor adds up to sixteen bands of your own anywhere
between 20 Hz and 20 kHz, each a bell, shelf, or pass filter with its own
frequency, gain (±20 dB), and Q. A bell's Q goes up to 16, about a twelfth of
an octave, narrow enough for a single resonance or a whine; shelves and pass
filters stop at 10, because past that they overshoot rather than narrow.

The two editors are tabs on the editing area's own border, and both edit the
same chain and the same graph: the sliders are eleven filters that happen to be
locked to the ladder, and an added band is one that is not. Nothing is
converted, approximated, or reconciled between the two views, so a change made
in either is exact and reversible.

Right-click a band slider for *Edit as Filter*, which moves that band into the
parametric editor without changing the sound.

** The response graph

The window plots the true combined magnitude response of the chain, computed
from the same coefficients the audio path renders with — not an interpolated
approximation. Drag a handle to change a band, double-click one to reset it,
or double-click empty space to add a parametric band exactly where you clicked.

The round handles are the graphic bands and the numbered nodes are the
parametric bands. Clicking or dragging either one shows the editor it belongs
to: a node opens the Parametric tab with its row selected, and a handle opens
the Graphic tab.

** The spectrum analyzer

A Hann-windowed FFT of the audio actually reaching your speakers is drawn
behind the response curve on the same frequency axis, with fast-attack /
slow-release smoothing, so you can read the programme material against your
shaping.

* The menu bar

CoreEQ runs as a menu bar application with no Dock icon. The status menu carries
the master switch, output device, the preset list, a live response preview, and
three Quick EQ tone controls (bass, mid, treble) layered over the active preset
— all without opening a window. It also holds *Open Equalizer*, *Settings…*,
and *Quit CoreEQ*: CoreEQ has no menu bar of its own, so this is where those live.

* Presets

Twenty-two built-in profiles — the classic set familiar from Apple Music and
Spotify — plus your own, managed from the sidebar. Built-ins are immutable;
your presets can be renamed, duplicated, edited, and deleted. Switching is
instantaneous and glitch-free. Hover a built-in preset, in the sidebar or the
menu, for a one-line description of what it is for. The sidebar's search field
matches the way the AutoEq catalog does: each word on its own, anywhere in the
name, so “boost” finds both boosters.

The =+= at the bottom of the sidebar creates a *New Preset* from the current
sound, opens the AutoEq catalog, or imports one. Right-click a preset for *Rename*, *Duplicate*, *Save
Changes*, *Reset to Preset*, *Export Preset*, and *Delete*.

Editing the active preset marks it *Edited*; *Save Changes* writes the values
into one of your own presets and *Revert* discards them. Your edits, the active
preset, and the on/off state are restored on every launch, per output device.

* Import and export

Presets move in and out as EqualizerAPO text — the format AutoEq writes its
headphone corrections in — or as CoreEQ's own =.coreeq= files. Import from the
=+= at the bottom of the sidebar, paste with =⌘V=, or drop a file onto the
preset list; every import shows what it found before anything is added. The
*AutoEq* button beside the =+= opens the catalog directly; for a correction from
autoeq.app by hand, use *Import by Hand…* in the catalog sheet.

An import that changes nothing asks a simple *Import* / *Cancel*. An import that
would change a value asks with a confirmation sheet instead: it lists each
change — which filter, the value as written and the value it would become, and
the CoreEQ limit responsible — and the measured response impact, as RMS and
maximum dB across 20 Hz–20 kHz. When a value has no physical response to
measure, the sheet says the impact cannot be measured rather than inventing a
number. Values CoreEQ can represent are kept exactly by default; a bell above
the ±12 dB graphic range can instead be clipped back onto its slider, and the
sheet shows what that would cost. Nothing is written until you confirm.

From EqualizerAPO text, CoreEQ reads =Preamp:= and =PK=, =LSC=, =HSC=, =HP= and
=LP= filters, with Q or bandwidth in octaves. It reads UTF-8, UTF-16 and
Windows-1252 files. A preset holds up to sixteen parametric bands with gain within
±20 dB, frequency 20 Hz–20 kHz, and Q 0.1–16 for a bell or 0.1–10 for shelves and
pass filters. Graphic bands and preamp stay within
±12 dB. A longer file keeps its pass filters and its strongest bands, and any
value brought into range is disclosed before it is added. Previously imported
presets whose gains were clamped must be reimported from the original file to
recover them. =GraphicEQ:=, =Channel:= and other EqualizerAPO commands are
skipped and listed in the preview — so filters meant for one channel apply to
both. A file without a =Preamp:= line gets the automatic trim.

Export is on a preset's right-click menu, as EqualizerAPO text or =.coreeq=.
Exporting the preset you are listening to writes what you hear, unsaved edits
included.

* The AutoEq catalog

CoreEQ can fetch a headphone correction from the [[https://github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq][AutoEq project]], without
downloading a file by hand. The *AutoEq* button beside the =+= opens the catalog
sheet directly, as does *Browse AutoEq Catalog…* in the =+= menu. For corrections AutoEq computes for a target you choose there,
use *Import by Hand…* in the sheet's action row: it closes the catalog, opens
the step-by-step guide on the main window, and the correction you paste lands
in your preset list.

The catalog sheet keeps the equalizer in context while you listen. Type in the
search field to find a model by name — the catalog holds thousands and matches
as you type. Each word is matched on its own, in any order, ignoring case,
accents and punctuation, so “sony mdr m1” finds Sony MDR-M1, and “600” finds
both the HD 600 and the HD600. Names where the words start a word are listed
first. A model measured more than once says how many measurements it has.

The *Measurement source* menu chooses who measured the headphones and how —
for example Oratory1990 or Rtings. Different measurements give different
corrections. Crinacle measurements are excluded from this catalog because
they are not available for redistribution.

Each measurement uses AutoEq's published, precomputed parametric correction.
Choose another compatible entry in *Target curve* to compute an alternative
correction locally. The browser credits Jaakko Pasanen and AutoEq, and names the
measurement source for the selected profile. *Import by Hand…* remains available
for corrections created on autoeq.app.

Selecting a model or measurement plays a preview after a short pause,
without saving anything, so you can hear it against your own music. *Cancel*
puts the previous sound back. *Import* converts the correction and keeps it as
one of your own presets, named after the model, where it can be edited and
exported like any other.

Closing the catalog and opening it again returns to where you were: the same
model, measurement and target, with the correction's curve drawn, and the same
search — or the model's name, if that search no longer lists it. It does not
play by itself — press *Play Preview*, click the model again, or choose
something else.

The catalog never refuses a correction CoreEQ can represent. When saving one
would change it to fit CoreEQ, the browser says so, and *Import* opens the same
confirmation sheet the manual imports use: each changed value with its response
impact, and the choice to keep it exact or clip a beyond-slider bell back into
the graphic range. If another filter occupies that slider, the clipped bell
remains a free filter at ±12 dB. Impact is measured at the active device rate.
The preview you hear is the correction kept exact, and a
correction that needs no change imports without a sheet.

No catalog or profiles are bundled with the app. Opening the AutoEq sheet for
the first time downloads the catalog and shows a progress bar; Cancel stops
the download and closes the sheet. Individual corrections download when
selected and are cached for later offline previews. A correction that has not
been downloaded still needs a connection; saved presets work independently.

CoreEQ checks for catalog updates when the sheet opens if the cache is at least
seven days old. The catalog and its profiles use the same Git commit so an
update cannot mix old and new corrections. If an update fails, CoreEQ keeps
the saved catalog and offers *Try Again*. With no network and no cached
catalog, the sheet offers *Try Again* or manual import.

Correction data comes from AutoEq by Jaakko Pasanen, used under the MIT licence;
each result names the measurement source it came from.

* Automatic gain

The preamp computes itself, and does so by default. Boosting makes a preset
louder, and louder always sounds better — which would make every comparison a
loudness test rather than a tone test. With *Auto* on, the trim follows the
chain and holds the preset at the loudness it started from, so what you hear
when you compare two presets is the shaping.

It is measured as the average lift across the audible range, weighted equally
per octave. A preset that lifts one narrow band is corrected gently; one that
lifts everything is corrected hard. Nineteen of the twenty-two built-ins lift
the chain on average, so most arrive with a small trim already applied.

The correction only ever attenuates. Cancelling a *cut* would mean raising
every frequency the preset deliberately left alone, which is a way to make
something clip that would not have clipped otherwise — so a preset that mostly
cuts is left quieter, as it was meant to be. To set the trim by hand,
drag the preamp slider: that switches *Auto* off, below it. Click *Auto* to
hand the trim back, or double-click the slider to return to the preset’s own
trim, as a band’s double-click returns it to the preset.

CoreEQ has no volume control. Its one level control is the *preamp*, which is
the same thing a hardware equalizer calls Level: makeup gain for what the curve
took away. Volume belongs to macOS, which offers it in the menu bar, on the
keyboard, and in Sound settings.

* A/B comparison

Two working states of one preset, switched from the header. Each holds a
complete sound — the preset, the chain, the trim, and the tone — so A/B compares
two versions rather than undoing one, and the pair belongs to the output device
like everything else you have not saved. Reaching for the other slot the first
time changes nothing: it starts as a copy of what you were hearing, and the first
difference is one you made.

* Per-device settings

The preset in use, any unsaved edits to it, and the preamp follow the *output
device*, the way volume does on macOS. Your headphone curve comes back when you
plug in headphones and gives way to the speaker curve when you unplug them. The
preset library itself is shared across devices.

* Settings

A gear in the window's header, or *Settings…* in the menu. Four tabs:
*General*, which holds *Open CoreEQ at login*, *Release the audio device when
nothing is playing*, *Pause while recording system audio*, and the status of
the System Audio Recording permission; *Exclusions*, the apps CoreEQ leaves
alone (see [[*Leaving apps out][Leaving apps out]]); *Diagnostics*, a report of the audio setup to
copy into a bug report; and *About*. Clicking the CoreEQ mark at the
top of the sidebar opens About directly.

* Recordings and other audio tools

A screen recording that captures system audio hears each app's original sound
and CoreEQ's equalized copy 27 ms later, which sounds like an echo. So CoreEQ
pauses itself while such a recording runs, and says so on the graph and in the
menu; *Pause while recording system audio* in Settings → General turns this
off. The first second or so of a recording can still be doubled — switch CoreEQ
off before recording if the start matters.

CoreEQ recognises recordings made through macOS's own screen capture, which is
what screen recorders and screen sharing in calls and browsers use. Recording
from a microphone is left alone, because it hears CoreEQ only once, through the
air, and so are Siri, dictation, and other apps that listen to the microphone.
A recorder that captures system audio another way — through a virtual device
such as BlackHole — is not recognised; switch CoreEQ off while using one.

Another tool that re-routes system audio, such as SoundSource, can double the
sound the same way when both run at once.

* Leaving apps out

Some apps should be heard exactly as they are — a DAW while mixing or
mastering, for one. Settings → *Exclusions* lists the apps CoreEQ leaves alone:
their sound goes straight to the output and never passes through CoreEQ, while
everything else stays equalized. This is not the same as a Flat preset, which
still takes the sound in and plays it back.

*Add…* chooses an app from the disk, so it can be excluded before it is even
open. Each listed app has a switch. Switched off, it stays on the list but is
equalized again, ready to switch back on. Select an app and click *Remove*, or
press Delete, to take it off the list. A change applies at once, with a
moment's break in the sound while CoreEQ rebuilds. An excluded app stays
excluded when it quits and is opened again.

Browsers can't be excluded. They play through a helper process that macOS
shares between apps, so leaving Safari out would mean leaving out every app
that shows web content. Apps that play their own sound — DAWs, music players,
video players — work.

Exclusion needs macOS 26 or later. On earlier versions the tab says so, and
every app is equalized.

* How it works

A global process tap captures the mixed output of every application, equalizes
it, and plays it back through the current output device. No =/Library/Audio=
component, no reboot, no per-application setup. Quit CoreEQ and system audio
returns to its normal path immediately.

You can check this yourself. Audio drivers live in one folder:

#+begin_src sh
ls /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/HAL
#+end_src

List it before and after installing CoreEQ: nothing is added. Anything there
was installed by other software, such as BlackHole. The device CoreEQ builds
while it runs is private to the app, so it does not appear in Audio MIDI Setup
either.

The engine rebuilds itself when the default output device changes, when the
sample rate changes, and after the machine wakes from sleep, with bounded
automatic retry on failure. Bypass is a real-time flag rather than a teardown,
so comparing processed and original sound is instantaneous. The render path
never allocates or blocks: parameter updates are staged behind an unfair lock
and picked up only when uncontended, and gain changes are smoothed over ~50 ms
so preset switches and slider moves never click or step.

* Limitations

What CoreEQ does not do, on purpose:

- *No volume control.* macOS already has one, in the menu bar, on the keyboard,
  and in Sound settings. CoreEQ's only level control is the preamp, which
  makes up for what the curve takes away.
- *Not bit-perfect.* CoreEQ works on the system mix, after macOS has mixed and
  resampled every app's audio. At Flat the sound passes through unchanged, and
  switched off CoreEQ leaves the audio path entirely, but it cannot give an app
  exclusive access to the device. The closest it comes is [[*Leaving apps out][leaving an app out]],
  which keeps CoreEQ out of that app's path altogether.
- *One curve for all channels.* On outputs with more than two channels (HDMI,
  DisplayPort, USB interfaces) every channel is equalized, with the same curve.
  There are no per-channel curves and no room correction.

And what it cannot do yet:

- Aggregate and Multi-Output Devices are not supported. Core Audio will not
  nest one aggregate device inside another, so CoreEQ refuses them and names the
  device and the reason rather than failing silently.
- On a device CoreEQ cannot capture stream by stream it falls back to a stereo
  mixdown, and channels beyond the pair the device calls stereo are silent.
  Settings → Diagnostics says when that has happened.
- Screen recordings can be doubled at their very start — see [[*Recordings and other audio tools][Recordings and
  other audio tools]].

* The audio permission

CoreEQ asks macOS whether it is allowed to process system audio, and says only
what applies. The same card appears over the response graph, in Settings →
General, and as a row in the menu bar.

| What CoreEQ says                              | What it means                                         | The button                                                                                       |
|-----------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| CoreEQ needs to reach the sound to change it   | macOS has not asked yet, or CoreEQ was just updated   | *Allow…* shows the macOS prompt                                                                  |
| CoreEQ isn’t allowed to reach the sound        | The permission was refused                            | *Open System Settings*; turn CoreEQ on there, then choose *Quit & Reopen* (or *Relaunch CoreEQ*)  |

CoreEQ never builds its audio path before the answer is yes.

macOS settles the permission when an app starts. Switching CoreEQ on or off in
System Settings does nothing for the copy already running — macOS offers to
quit and reopen it — so a change takes effect on the next launch.

* Troubleshooting

*The fastest route to an answer is the diagnostics report*, in Settings →
Diagnostics → Copy. It names the output device and what CoreEQ built around
it, whether audio is reaching CoreEQ, how often the engine has restarted and
why, and whether the output clipped. Once you have opened the AutoEq catalog,
it also names the catalog version CoreEQ has cached and why the last load
failed, if it did. Attach it to any bug report.

- *CoreEQ says it isn’t allowed to reach the sound.* Allow CoreEQ under Screen &
  System Audio Recording in System Settings → Privacy & Security, then choose
  *Quit & Reopen* when macOS asks, or *Relaunch CoreEQ* on the card.
- *The permission is on in System Settings and CoreEQ still hears nothing.*
  Turn CoreEQ off and on again in System Settings and relaunch it, or clear its
  entry from Terminal and relaunch it, which makes it ask again:
  =tccutil reset AudioCapture com.andreypudov.coreeq=
- *No sound, or something sounds wrong, and you want to rule CoreEQ out.* Switch
  it off, or quit it: system audio returns to its normal path immediately.
- *A screen recording has an echo.* See [[*Recordings and other audio tools][Recordings and other audio tools]].
- *An output device is refused.* Aggregate and Multi-Output Devices are not
  supported yet; choose a single output device.
